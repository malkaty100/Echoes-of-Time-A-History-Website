How It Began

A City Divided

The story of the Berlin Wall began at the end of World War II. In 1945, Nazi Germany was defeated by the Allied powers. Germany was divided into four occupation zones, controlled by the United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union. Berlin, the capital of Germany, was also divided into four sectors, even though the city was located deep inside the Soviet occupation zone. At first, the division was not intended to create a permanent border. The Allied powers hoped that Germany could eventually be rebuilt and governed peacefully. However, serious political disagreements soon developed between the Soviet Union and the Western Allies. The Soviet Union supported a communist political and economic system, while the United States, Britain, and France supported democratic governments and market economies. These differences became increasingly difficult to overcome and eventually led to the division of Germany. In 1949, two separate German states were officially established. The western occupation zones became the Federal Republic of Germany, commonly known as West Germany. The Soviet occupation zone became the German Democratic Republic, or East Germany. Berlin remained divided as well. West Berlin was connected politically and economically to West Germany, while East Berlin became the capital of East Germany. The divided city soon became one of the most important symbols of the growing conflict between East and West.

What Happened to People Who Tried to Escape?

Many people attempted to cross from East Berlin to West Berlin. Some used tunnels, hidden compartments, ropes, or other creative methods. Escaping was extremely dangerous, and people caught attempting to cross could be arrested.

Life Behind the Iron Curtain

During the 1950s, life in East and West Germany became increasingly different. West Germany experienced rapid economic growth and developed closer relationships with the Western European countries and the United States. East Germany followed a centrally planned communist economy under the influence of the Soviet Union. The differences between the two systems were especially visible in Berlin. Although the city was divided, people could still move between many parts of East and West Berlin. This made it possible for East German citizens to see the differences between the two sides for themselves. Large numbers of people began leaving East Germany for the West. Many traveled through Berlin because it was one of the few places where crossing from the communist East to the West was relatively easy. Between 1949 and 1961, millions of people left East Germany. They included workers, students, professionals, engineers, doctors, teachers, and other young people. The loss of so many citizens created serious economic and political problems for the East German government. The government tried different measures to stop people from leaving, but these efforts were not enough. By 1961, East German leaders decided that a much stronger physical barrier was necessary.

Families and Communities Separated

The construction of the wall had an immediate effect on ordinary people. Families who lived on opposite sides of Berlin could suddenly no longer visit each other freely. Some people discovered that their usual route to work was blocked. Others were separated from relatives who lived only a few streets away. Streets, train lines, and neighborhoods that had once been connected were divided by the new border. The wall also changed the physical appearance of Berlin. Buildings that stood close to the border were sometimes demolished or sealed, while streets and public spaces were redesigned around the new security system. For many Berliners, the wall became a constant part of everyday life. It was impossible to ignore. People could see it from their homes, workplaces, schools, and streets. Although the wall was intended to prevent people from leaving East Germany, some people continued to attempt dangerous escapes. Over the years, people tried to cross the border in many different ways, including through tunnels, hidden compartments, and other carefully planned routes.
